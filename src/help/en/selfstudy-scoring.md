### 1. Questions Excluded from Self-study
In self-study, essay-type subjective questions (Essay questions) are not scored.

### 2. Types of Scoring Methods
Scoring methods can be divided into four types based on whether there is negative marking and whether partial credit is given.

- 2.1. **Scoring with negative marking and partial credit:** Negative marking and partial credit apply only to objective questions; short answer subjective questions have no partial credit and are not penalized for incorrect answers.
    + 2.1.1. **Single-choice objective question**
        * 2.1.1.1. Correct answer: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.1.1.2. No answer or multiple answers: **0 points** are awarded.
        * 2.1.1.3. One wrong answer: **A deduction of assigned points / (number of choices - 1)** is applied.
    + 2.1.2. **Multiple-choice objective question**
        * 2.1.2.1. Correct number of answers and all selected are correct: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.1.2.2. Number of selected answers differs from correct count: **0 points** are awarded.
        * 2.1.2.3. Correct number of answers selected: **(number of wrong options x number of selected correct answers - number of correct options x number of selected wrong answers) x assigned points / number of choices points** are awarded.
- 2.2. **Scoring with negative marking and without partial credit:** Negative marking applies only to objective questions; short answer subjective questions are not penalized for incorrect answers.
    + 2.2.1. **Single-choice objective question**
        * 2.2.1.1. Correct answer: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.2.1.2. No answer or multiple answers: **0 points **are awarded.
        * 2.2.1.3. One wrong answer: **A deduction of assigned points / (number of choices - 1)** is applied.
    + 2.2.2. **Multiple-choice objective question**
        * 2.2.2.1. Correct number of answers and all selected are correct: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.2.2.2. Number of selected answers differs from correct count: **0 points** are awarded.
        * 2.2.2.3. Correct number of answers selected but contains errors or omissions: **A deduction of assigned points / (number of choices x (number of choices - 1))** is applied.
- 2.3. **Scoring without negative marking and with partial credit**
    + 2.3.1. **Single-choice objective question**
        * 2.3.1.1. Correct answer: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.3.1.2. Otherwise: **0 points** are awarded.
    + 2.3.2. **Multiple-choice objective question**
        * 2.3.2.1. Correct number of answers and all selected are correct: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.3.2.2. Number of selected answers differs from correct count: **0 points** are awarded.
        * 2.3.2.3. Correct number of answers selected: **assigned points x number of selected correct answers / number of correct options points** are awarded.
- 2.4. **Scoring without negative marking and without partial credit**
    + 2.4.1. **Single-choice objective question**
        * 2.4.1.1. Correct answer: **Assigned points **are awarded.
        * 2.4.1.2. Otherwise: **0 points** are awarded.
    + 2.4.2. **Multiple-choice objective question**
        * 2.4.2.1. Correct number of answers and all selected are correct: **Assigned points** are awarded.
        * 2.4.2.2. Otherwise: **0 points** are awarded.

### 3. Sharing Scoring Rule Settings
Since the scoring rule settings for self-study share values with the exam scope settings in exam creation, changing the exam scope settings will automatically update the self-study scoring rule settings.

### 4. Usefulness of Negative Marking
- 4.1. An exam is a tool to evaluate a student **Ability**, not their **luck**.
- 4.2. In exams, negative marking is often avoided because it gives the impression of being overly harsh. However, from the perspective of an evaluator who must strictly assess student ability, failing to use it means **luck** is evaluated **alongside skill**, **acting as noise that hinders accurate assessment.** Evaluations without negative marking have a blind spot where accuracy is reduced. For an extreme example, in a 40-question single-choice exam with 4 options per question, a student who guesses every answer without any knowledge would be expected to get about 10 questions right and 30 wrong. This results in a 25% accuracy rate purely by luck. **A student who knows nothing would receive 25 points, which is significant evaluation noise.**
- 4.3. **An appropriate penalty can offset this luck-based noise to a significant degree, if not perfectly.** Consider the same 40-question exam: guessing has a 1/4 (25%) chance of being right and 3/4 (75%) chance of being wrong. If a correct answer awards 2.5 points and an incorrect answer deducts 0.83 points (= 2.5 / 3), a student guessing all 40 questions would **converge toward a score of 0.** It is only natural for someone who knows nothing to receive 0 points. If the calculated score is negative, a score of 0 is given, as there are no negative final scores.
- 4.4. Negative marking also **automatically evaluates partial knowledge**, such as when a student knows for certain that one out of four options is wrong and guesses among the remaining three. In the same 40-question exam, if a student consistently eliminates one option and guesses among the remaining three, they would be expected to get about 13 right and 27 wrong. This is because the probability of being right is 1/3 (33.3%) and wrong is 2/3 (66.7%). With a 2.5 point reward and 0.83 point deduction, this student"s score would converge toward approximately 11.2 points. **The ability to recognize what is definitely not the answer is thus realistically valued at 11.2 points.**
- 4.5. This discussion assumes the application of the **Law of Large Numbers** in statistics. In many practical cases, however, students do not guess a large enough number of questions for the **Law of Large Numbers to apply**. Nevertheless, in such cases, students—using a 4-option question as an example—are three times more likely to lose 1 point than to gain 3 points by guessing, and thus **choose a strategy of leaving the answer blank.** This results in the students themselves excluding evaluation noise. Statistically, leaving a blank guarantees 0 points with 100% probability, whereas guessing has an expected value of 0 but a standard deviation greater than 0; therefore, **a guessing strategy is by no means a wise choice.**
